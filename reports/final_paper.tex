\begin{abstract}
Our Project explores refugee migration rates and how we can predict such rates based on origin country and asylum country freedom indexes. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Data}
\label{Data}
The data in our project consists of refugee migration statistics from United Nations High Commission for Refugees and Freedom Indexes from the Freedom House. With this Data we plan to investigate refugee rates from countries around the world, and eventually create a model that predicts migration rates based on the freedom indexes of ones home and host country.

The Freedom in the World dataset, published annually by Freedom House, provides scores and ratings from 2013 to 2021 for countries and territories. The data include measures such as political rights ratings, civil liberties ratings, and aggregate scores for subcategories like electoral process, political pluralism and participation, rule of law, and freedom of expression and belief. Each country-year entry also includes an overall status of Free, Partly Free, or Not Free. These variables allow us to capture the political and civil environment in both origin and asylum countries.

The UNHCR Persons of Concern dataset contains annual records beginning in 2004, organized by year, country of origin, and country of asylum. For each country pair, the dataset reports counts of various displaced groups, including refugees, asylum-seekers, internally displaced persons (IDPs), stateless persons, and others of concern. These values reflect the scale of displacement across borders and within states. These values provide the dependent variable in our analysis, which is the amount of refugee movement from one country to another in a given year.

The unit of analysis in our study is the country-year pair, which links refugee flows from an origin country to an asylum country in a given year with their corresponding freedom index values. This allows us to test hypotheses about how political repression, democratic governance, and civil liberties shape refugee migration. While the UNHCR data captures migration outcomes, the Freedom House data provides measures of the political conditions that may act as push and pull factors.

These datasets do consist limitations, such that refugee statistics often underrepresented the quantities of undocumented displacement, and the Freedom House scores, while widely used, are based partly on subjective assessments. Despite this, their examination provides a unique opportunity to connect quantitative migration patterns with qualitative assessments of political freedom.

\subsection{Data Cleaning}
The data cleaning process for the refugee and freedom index data involved several steps to ensure the data was ready for analysis. For the persons-of-concern.csv file, which contains the data regarding refugee migration, the initial loading and inspection revealed no immediate issues with missing values or data types, so no further cleaning was required. For the All-data-FIW-2013-2021.xlsx file, which contains the data regarding freedom indexes, initial attempts to load the data resulted in incorrect headers due to metadata at the beginning of the sheet. This was corrected by specifying the correct sheet name ('FIW13-21') and header row (row 0) during loading. The columns were then renamed for clarity, specifically 'C/T?' to 'Country Type', 'Country/Territory' to 'Country', and 'Total' to 'Freedom Total Score'. Finally, the first row, which contained descriptive information rather than data. We removed this so data handling could be smoother. Then relevant columns were selected and converted to appropriate numeric types. Finally we checked for null values and confirmed that there were no missing values in the selected columns after these steps.

\section{Methods and Results}
\label{methods}

\subsection{Method Overview}
Our primary objective is to develop a predictive model that quantifies the relationship between political and civil liberties (push and pull factors) and refugee migration flows. This task is framed as a \textbf{regression problem}, where the dependent variable is the count of refugees from a given origin country to an asylum country in a specific year. The core methodology involves three phases: feature engineering and data integration, predictive model selection and training, and robust model validation. Due to the inherent non-linear and potentially discontinuous nature of political conditions and their impact on human migration, we employ a non-parametric approach. Specifically, we will use the aggregated total freedom scores of the origin and asylum countries as the key independent variables (features) to predict the total number of refugees between them.

\subsubsection*{Feature Engineering and Data Integration}
To align the two datasets---the time-series refugee counts from UNHCR and the annual freedom index scores from Freedom House---we performed a key data integration step. For every unique country-year observation in the UNHCR data (defined by $\text{Year}$, $\text{Country of Origin ISO}$, and $\text{Country of Asylum ISO}$), we joined the corresponding freedom scores from the Freedom House data. This join was executed twice:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item To associate the \textbf{Origin Country}'s $\text{Total}$ freedom score ($F_{\text{Origin}}$) based on the $\text{Year}$ and $\text{Country of Origin}$. The Origin Country's score is hypothesized to act as a \textbf{push factor} (low score predicts high migration).
    \item To associate the \textbf{Asylum Country}'s $\text{Total}$ freedom score ($F_{\text{Asylum}}$) based on the $\text{Year}$ and $\text{Country of Asylum}$. The Asylum Country's score is hypothesized to act as a \textbf{pull factor} (high score predicts high migration).
\end{enumerate}
The target variable, $\text{Refugees}$, is a count and is highly skewed, with many zero or very small values. To normalize this distribution and stabilize the variance for the regression model, we applied a logarithmic transformation: $Y = \log(1 + \text{Refugees})$. The addition of $1$ ensures that zero refugee counts can be handled without resulting in undefined logarithms. The final engineered dataset consists of the features $(F_{\text{Origin}}, F_{\text{Asylum}})$ and the target variable $Y$.

\subsection{Model Justification}
We selected the \textbf{Random Forest Regressor (RFR)} as our primary predictive model. [Image of a Decision Tree structure]. This choice is justified by several advantages over traditional linear models:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Non-linearity and Interactions:} The relationship between political freedom and migration is complex; it is unlikely to be a simple linear function. The RFR, an ensemble learning method based on decision trees, can inherently capture non-linear relationships and intricate interaction effects between the origin and asylum country scores without requiring manual feature engineering.
    \item \textbf{Robustness to Data Characteristics:} RFR is robust against outliers and requires minimal data preprocessing, specifically avoiding the need for feature scaling, which is beneficial when combining features with different inherent scales (like the aggregated freedom scores).
    \item \textbf{Feature Importance:} As an added benefit, the RFR provides a quantitative measure of \textbf{feature importance}, allowing us to determine whether the push factors ($F_{\text{Origin}}$) or the pull factors ($F_{\text{Asylum}}$) have a greater predictive influence on the migration rate.
\end{enumerate}


\subsection{Model Training Procedure}
\subsubsection*{Data Splitting}
The engineered dataset was randomly partitioned into training and testing sets using an 80/20 split. The training set (80\% of the data) was used to train the Random Forest model, and the held-out test set (20\%) was reserved exclusively for final, unbiased evaluation of the model's performance on unseen data. While a temporal split is possible for time-series data, a random split was chosen to maximize the diversity of country-year observations in both the training and testing phases.


\subsubsection*{Hyperparameter Optimization}
To prevent overfitting and optimize the model’s generalization ability, we performed \textbf{cross-validated hyperparameter tuning}. We employed a 5-fold cross-validation scheme coupled with a Randomized Search to efficiently explore the hyperparameter space. Key hyperparameters targeted for optimization included:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf{n\_\text{estimators}}$: The number of decision trees in the forest.
    \item $\mathbf{max\_\text{depth}}$: The maximum depth of each individual decision tree.
    \item $\mathbf{min\_\text{samples\_split}}$: The minimum number of samples required to split an internal node.
\end{itemize}
The model's performance during cross-validation was evaluated using the negative Mean Squared Error (MSE) on the log-transformed target, with the goal of selecting the parameter combination that minimizes this error.


\subsection{Model Validation Plan}
The trained and tuned Random Forest Regressor will be evaluated on the held-out test set using both statistical and domain-specific metrics.

\subsubsection*{Statistical Metrics (Log Scale)}
The primary statistical metrics calculated on the log-transformed target variable ($Y$) and its prediction ($\hat{Y}$) are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $\mathbf{R^2}$ \textbf{(Coefficient of Determination)}: Measures the proportion of the variance in the dependent variable that is predictable from the independent variables. A value closer to $1.0$ indicates a better fit.
    \item \textbf{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)}: Provides a measure of the average magnitude of the error in the $\log(1 + \text{Refugees})$ space.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{Domain-Specific Metrics (Original Scale)}
To ensure the model's predictions are interpretable in the context of real-world refugee counts, the predictions $\hat{Y}$ will be inverse-transformed back to the original scale using the formula: $$\text{Predicted Refugees} = \exp(\hat{Y}) - 1$$ The model's final performance will be assessed using:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE)}: Calculates the average absolute difference between the true number of refugees and the predicted number. This metric is easily interpretable, providing the typical error in the units of refugees.
    \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE)}: Measures the accuracy as a percentage of the error relative to the true values. This is particularly useful for assessing predictive power across different scales of migration flows.
\end{enumerate}
