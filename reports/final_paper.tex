\begin{abstract}
Our Project explores refugee migration rates and how we can predict such rates based on origin country, intensity of conflict, and asylum country freedom indexes. 
\end{abstract}


\section{Data}
\label{Data}
The data in our project consists of refugee migration statistics from the United Nations High Commission for Refugees (UNHCR), Freedom Indexes from the Freedom House (FIW), and the UCDP Priority Conflict Data. With this data, we plan to investigate refugee rates from countries around the world, and eventually create a model that predicts migration rates based on the political freedom and conflict intensity of the home and host countries.

\subsection{Data Sources}

\subsubsection*{Freedom in the World (FIW) Index}
The Freedom in the World dataset, published annually by Freedom House, provides scores and ratings from 2013 to 2021 for countries and territories. The data include measures such as political rights ratings, civil liberties ratings, and aggregate scores for subcategories like electoral process, political pluralism and participation, rule of law, and freedom of expression and belief. Each country-year entry also includes an overall status of Free, Partly Free, or Not Free. These variables allow us to capture the political and civil environment in both origin and asylum countries.

\subsubsection*{UCDP Priority Conflict Data}
To better capture the extreme push factors driving displacement, we integrated the \texttt{UcdpPrioConflict\_v25\_1.csv} dataset, which records annual data on organized state-based conflict. This data provides the \texttt{intensity\_level} (1 for minor conflict, 2 for war), allowing us to quantify the severity of active violence. The UCDP data is primarily used to generate conflict metrics for the asylum country, such as total annual and cumulative conflict intensity, serving as critical predictors of migration flow.

\subsubsection*{UNHCR Persons of Concern Data}
The UNHCR Persons of Concern dataset contains annual records beginning in 2004, organized by year, country of origin, and country of asylum. For each country pair, the dataset reports counts of various displaced groups, including refugees, asylum-seekers, internally displaced persons (IDPs), stateless persons, and others of concern. These values reflect the scale of displacement across borders and within states. These values provide the dependent variable in our analysis, which is the amount of refugee movement from one country to another in a given year.

The unit of analysis in our study is the country-pair-year observation, which links refugee flows from an origin country to an asylum country in a given year with their corresponding freedom index values and conflict intensity measures. This allows us to test hypotheses about how political repression, democratic governance, civil liberties, and active conflict shape refugee migration. While the UNHCR data captures migration outcomes, the Freedom House and UCDP data provide measures of the political and violence conditions that act as push and pull factors.

These datasets do consist limitations, such that refugee statistics often underrepresented the quantities of undocumented displacement, and the Freedom House scores, while widely used, are based partly on subjective assessments. Additionally, the UCDP data focuses only on organized, state-based violence. Despite this, their examination provides a unique opportunity to connect quantitative migration patterns with qualitative assessments of political freedom and conflict.


\subsection{Data Cleaning}
Data cleaning was performed across all three datasets to ensure readiness for integration and analysis. The UNHCR Persons of Concern data required only initial inspection, as its integrity was confirmed. For the Freedom in the World (FIW) data, preparation involved loading with specified sheet/header, renaming columns for clarity (e.g., \texttt{Total} to \texttt{Freedom Total Score}), dropping a descriptive row, and converting relevant columns to numeric types. The UCDP Priority Conflict Data required the most specialized preparation, involving data aggregation by country and year to sum the \texttt{intensity\_level} and generate country-year level features (e.g., \texttt{asylum\_conflict\_total\_conflict\_intensity}) for the Random Forest Regressor model.

\section{Methods and Results}
\label{methods}

\subsection{Method Overview}
The primary objective of this project is to create a robust predictive model that quantifies the relationship between political/civil liberties, active conflict, and refugee migration flows. We frame our task as a regression problem, where the dependent variable is the count of refugees from a given origin country to an asylum country in a specific year. The core methodology involves three phases: feature engineering and data integration, predictive model training (Random Forest Regressor), and model validation and assessment. Given the complex, non-linear, and multi-faceted nature of conflict and political stability affecting human migration, we employ a non-parametric approach using a diverse set of features.

\subsubsection*{Feature Engineering and Data Integration}

The model integrates data from three primary sources:
\begin{samepage}
\begingroup
\setlength{\emergencystretch}{1em}
\sloppy

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{UNHCR Persons of Concern} (\texttt{persons\_of\_concern.csv}): Provides the target variable, \textbf{Refugees}.
    \item \textbf{Freedom in the World (FIW) Index} (\texttt{All\_data\_FIW\_2013-2021.xlsx}): Provides political/civil liberties scores.
    \item \textbf{UCDP Priority Conflict Data} (\texttt{UcdpPrioConflict\_v25\_1.csv}): Provides annual conflict intensity and cumulative intensity data.
\end{enumerate}

\endgroup
\end{samepage}

To create the final analytic dataset of approximately 99,946 unique country–pair–year observations (2013–2021), we performed a series of feature engineering and integration steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{FIW Integration (Push and Pull Factors):} We joined the FIW scores twice to capture the \textbf{Origin Country's} score ($F_{\text{Origin}}$) as a \textbf{push factor} (low freedom score predicts high migration) and the \textbf{Asylum Country's} score ($F_{\text{Asylum}}$) as a \textbf{pull factor} (high freedom score predicts high immigration).

    \item \textbf{Conflict Feature Generation:} The UCDP data was aggregated and merged to derive conflict indicators primarily for the Asylum country, hypothesized to diminish its pull factor, specifically the annual sum of active conflict intensity (\textbf{\texttt{asylum\_conflict\_total\break\_conflict\_intensity}}) and the total sum of historical conflict intensity (\textbf{\texttt{asylum\_conflict\_total\break\_cumulative\_intensity}}).

    \item \textbf{Differential Feature Creation:} A crucial engineered characteristic, \textbf{\texttt{Freedom\_Score\_Difference}} ($F_{\text{Origin}} - F_{\text{Asylum}}$), was calculated to holistically capture the political disparity between the two countries, which is hypothesized to be a strong determinant of the flow magnitude.

    \item \textbf{Target Transformation:} The target variable, $\textbf{Refugees}$, is highly biased. To achieve a more normal distribution and stabilize the variance of the regression model, we applied a logarithmic transformation: $Y = \log(1 + \text{Refugees})$. The final data set engineered consists of the full set of characteristics (FIW scores, conflict metrics, difference scores, and other contextual variables such \texttt{as} asylum seekers) and the target variable $Y$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Model Justification: Random Forest Regressor}
We selected a \textbf{Random Forest regression (RFR)} as our predictive model. A sample model can be seen in the figure below. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \includegraphics[width=3.2in]{reports/figures/Random_Forest_Regression.png}
    \centering
    \caption{Sample Random Forest Regression Model}
    \label{sample model}
\end{figure}

This choice is justified by its suitability for the complex and high-dimensional nature of our features:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Handling Non-linearity and Interactions:} The RFR, an ensemble method based on decision trees, can inherently capture highly non-linear relationships and intricate interaction effects among the diverse features (e.g., how the effect of **conflict intensity** in the asylum country is conditional on the origin country's freedom score). This avoids the need for manual specification of complex interaction terms.
    \item \textbf{Robustness to Mixed Data:} RFR is robust against outliers and effectively handles a mix of continuous, categorical, and count data. This is particularly beneficial when combining disparate features like raw population counts (\texttt{asylum} seekers), ordinal ratings (FIW scores), and aggregated intensity metrics (UCDP conflict).
    \item \textbf{Feature Importance and Interpretability:} The RFR provides a quantitative measure of \textbf{feature importance}, which is critical to the interpretability of the model. This allows us to definitively rank and assess the influence of conflict intensity, political freedom differences, and traditional asylum-seeker counts on migration rates. The significance analysis revealed that the main drivers were \texttt{asylum\_conflict\_total\break\_conflict\_intensity} (0.178), \texttt{asylum} seekers (0.156) and \texttt{asylum\_conflict\_total\break\_cumulative\_intensity} (0.128).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Model Training Procedure}
\subsubsection*{Data Splitting}
The engineered dataset was partitioned into training and testing sets using an 80/20 random split. The 80\% training set was used to fit the RFR model, while the 20\% held-out test set was reserved exclusively for final, unbiased evaluation of the model's performance on unseen data. A random split was chosen over a strict temporal split to ensure maximum diversity of country-year observations in both sets, which helps the model generalize across various geopolitical conditions and time points.

\subsubsection*{Hyperparameter Optimization}
To optimize the model’s generalization performance and mitigate overfitting, we performed cross-validated hyperparameter tuning. We utilized a 5-fold cross-validation and a Randomized Search to efficiently explore the parameter space. Key hyperparameters optimized included:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$\mathbf{n\_{estimators}}$}: The number of decision trees in the forest.
    \item $\mathbf{max\_{depth}}$: The maximum depth of each individual decision tree.
    \item $\mathbf{min\_{samples\_split}}$: The minimum number of samples required to split an internal node.
\end{itemize}
The model's performance during cross-validation was evaluated using the negative Mean Squared Error (MSE) on the log-transformed target, with the objective of selecting the parameter combination that minimizes MSE.

\subsection{Model Validation Plan}
The trained Random Forest Regressor will be evaluated on the withheld test set using both statistical metrics (on the log-scale) and domain-specific metrics (on the original count scale).

\subsubsection*{Statistical Metrics (Log Scale)}
The primary statistical metrics calculated on the log-transformed target variable ($Y$) and its prediction ($\hat{Y}$) are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbf{R^2}$ \textbf{Score}: Measures the proportion of variance in the log-transformed refugee counts that is predictable from the features.
    \item \textbf{Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)}: Measures the magnitude of the errors on the log scale.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Domain-Specific Metrics (Original Scale)}
To ensure the model's predictions are interpretable in the context of real-world refugee counts, the predictions $\hat{Y}$ will be inverse-transformed back to the original scale using the formula:
\begin{center}
\begin{scriptsize}
$$\text{Predicted Refugees} = \exp(\hat{Y}) - 1$$
\end{scriptsize}
\end{center}
The model's final performance, relevant for practical application, will be assessed using:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Error (MAE)}: Provides the average magnitude of error in terms of raw refugee counts.
    \item \textbf{Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE)}: Provides a percentage error metric, though this can be sensitive to low actual count values.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{conclusion}

\subsection{Project Summary and Key Findings}

The primary objective of this data science project was to create a model for predicting global refugee numbers, with the goal of finding valuable insights for humanitarian resource planning and policy development. Our analysis successfully confirmed the hypothesis that a combination of governance quality (FIW scores) and regional security (UCDP conflict data) are measurable drivers of displacement.

The trained Random Forest Regressor model yielded an $R^2$ score of $0.532$ and a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of $2,449.43$ refugees on the held-out test set. While not reaching perfect predictive capability, this performance level indicates meaningful feature importance and model validity.

The feature importance analysis, a major strength of the Random Forest methodology, provided clear, actionable insights The total conflict intensity in the country of asylum (\texttt{asylum\_conflict\_total\_conflict\_intensity}, importance: $0.178$) was the most influential feature. This suggests that the immediate security of the given region often outweighs the specific degree of conflict in the origin country for a new arrival, or it effectively captures regional displacement trends.

The number of existing Asylum-seekers \texttt{Asylum\_seekers} (importance: $0.156$) was highly predictive, acting as a crucial proxy for established migration infrastructure and ongoing crises. The relative freedom metric, \texttt{Freedom\_Score\_Difference} (importance: $0.107$), confirmed the expectation that displacement is influenced by the perceived democratic deficit between the country of origin and the asylum country.
\end{itemize}

These results confirm that migration patterns are multivariate, driven by a balance of push factors (conflict, lack of freedom) and pull factors (regional stability and existing displacement networks).

\subsection{Defense Against Criticism and Model Limitations}

The main point of critique for this project is often the performance metrics, particularly the $R^2$ score of $0.532$ and the large Mean Squared Error (MSE) of $492,355,882.55$. These numbers, though seemingly modest, are justifiable given the context of the problem:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Complexity of Social Phenomena:} Unlike models built on deterministic physical processes, predicting human migration is inherently subject to high variance due to unquantifiable factors, such as abrupt policy changes, social media-driven political movements, or unpredictable local events. Achieving an $R^2$ that accounts for over $53\%$ of the variance in this chaotic, real-world environment demonstrates significant validity and utility.
    \item \textbf{Data Quality and Sparsity:} The project faced substantial challenges in data preparation . The merging of three independent datasets introduced significant data sparsity and missing values, especially when aligning country-year pairs. Given that the final model was trained on data that required numerous rows with missing values to be dropped or simplified, the model's performance is, in part, a reflection of the available public data quality, rather than a failing of the modeling technique itself. The complexity of integrating political, conflict, and humanitarian records from different organizations inherently limits the achievable upper bound of accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Interpretability as Priority:} The choice of the Random Forest Regressor was a conscious design decision. While more complex, black-box models like deep learning networks might potentially achieve a slightly higher $R^2$, the Random Forest provides clear, localized feature importance scores. For a policy-driven model, understanding *why* a prediction is being made (e.g., "high conflict intensity in the receiving region is the primary driver") is critically more important than achieving a marginal gain in pure numerical accuracy.
\end{enumerate}

In defense, the project's success lies not just in its predictive score, but in its ability to provide an interpretable, explainable baseline that policymakers can trust and act upon, validating the influence of governance and conflict metrics.

\subsection{Suggestions for Additional Work Outside the Scope}

To further enhance the predictive power and utility of this model, the following avenues of research, which were outside the scope of the current project, are recommended:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Advanced Imputation and Data Enrichment:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Instead of using simple zero-filling or row dropping for missing data, implementing Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE) or K-Nearest Neighbors (kNN) imputation should be explored. These methods generate more realistic estimates of missing values, potentially stabilizing the model and enriching the feature set.
        \item Integrate Economic Indicators such as GDP per capita and income inequality from the World Bank and Climate Data. Economic and environmental stress are recognized as powerful, indirect drivers of displacement that are currently absent from the model.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Alternative Modeling and Temporal Dynamics:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Evaluation of Gradient Boosting Machines (GBMs): Experiment with state-of-the-art models like XGBoost or LightGBM. These boosting methods often outperform Random Forests on structured data and may capture non-linearities the current model misses.
        \item Time Series Integration: The current model is predominantly cross-sectional. Future work must explicitly incorporate the time series nature of the data by using lagged features. A full Recurrent Neural Network (RNN) or ARIMA-type model could be deployed to leverage the sequential dependency in refugee flows.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Classification for Policy Utility:}
   

   \begin{itemize}
        \item  Shift the modeling goal from a regression task to a classification task, where we would instead be predicting the severity level. This policy-focused output is often more valuable for practical resource allocation and the creation of an early-warning risk indicator system than a highly precise numerical prediction.
    \end{itemize}
    
\end{enumerate}
This project established a verifiable connection between sociopolitical indicators and forced displacement. The pathway for future research offers a clear trajectory to evolve this foundational work into a highly accurate and actionable tool to assist with humanitarian forecasting.
